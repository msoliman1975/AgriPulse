Frost prediction at grid‑cell level for Brazilian coffee

A frost decision tree already runs on this platform. It is scoped to potato and it reads one farm‑wide forecast minimum. Coffee at farm level is roughly one day of work on top of it. Coffee per cell is a different build, because the tree engine can evaluate a cell but the weather it reads is the same number for every cell on the farm. This page lists each gap, its cost, and the accuracy expected once it closes.

Absent

  • Any coffee frost tree

  • Dew point — not requested, not stored
  • Any digital elevation model. Searched for SRTM, Copernicus DEM, NASADEM, terrain. No match.
  • Terrain columns on grid_cells. It has geometry, centroid, area, nothing else.
  • Soil temperature and soil moisture
  • Canopy height
The one fact the whole build turns on The tree engine can already evaluate a tree once per grid cell. The weather it hands that tree cannot. WeatherSnapshot is documented as “all weather inputs needed for one block evaluation” and every scope it carries is keyed on farm_id. Point a cell‑scoped frost tree at forecast_72h.air_temp_c_min today and all 4,000 cells return the same answer. The missing piece is a per‑cell temperature, not a per‑cell evaluation.
Why the weather feed cannot supply it Open‑Meteo serves a 9 km grid, one point per farm. A cell is 20 m to 5000 m. The forecast carries no information at that scale and no provider has it. The per‑cell number has to be derived from terrain.

3 · Expected accuracy

What each stage buys

Error is the root mean square error of predicted night minimum temperature, in degrees Celsius, at a 24‑hour horizon. Hit rate is the share of nights where the frost or no‑frost call is correct. Note that moving from the farm point to the cell first costs accuracy: the terrain step spreads one calibrated number across many cells, and only ground truth wins it back.

These are expected values, not measured ones. They come from published frost studies in Korea, California and the western United States, scaled to a 9 km driving forecast. Stage 0 is what the potato tree does on this platform now, but its error has never been measured against Brazilian observations. Treat every row as the size of the prize, not as a promise. The first honest number arrives after one Brazilian winter of forecast‑versus‑observed comparison, which weather_forecasts already stores every issuance for.

The 0.65 °C figure is the root mean square error FAO reports for its own worked example of the two‑hours‑after‑sunset equation. It applies to a single instrumented point on radiative frost nights, not to a whole farm.

4 · Method

How a cell gets its own number

The offset is static. Terrain does not change, so it is computed once when the grid is created and stored on the cell. Runtime cost per night is one addition per cell.

  1. Load a digital elevation model over the farm. Copernicus DEM GLO‑30 is free, global, 30 m, and covers Brazil.
  2. Per cell, compute elevation, slope, aspect, topographic position index and height above the nearest drainage. Store them on grid_cells.
  3. Reduce those to one signed number, frost_offset_c. A low sheltered cell gets a negative value, a mid‑slope cell near zero. Seed the coefficients from published cold‑air‑pooling work, then calibrate.
  4. Expose it to the condition language as a terrain source, so a tree leaf can compare forecast_72h.air_temp_c_min plus terrain.frost_offset_c against a threshold. This is the step that makes scope: cell mean something for weather.
  5. Seed a coffee frost tree from the potato one, with Arabica thresholds and scope: cell.
  6. Suppress the offsets when night wind is above about 2 m s‑¹ or cloud cover is high. That night is advective, and the whole farm freezes evenly.
  7. Weight the damage text by plant age from planting_date. A young plant sits inside the coldest 50 cm of air; a mature plant keeps its upper canopy above it.
Resolution honesty A 30 m elevation model cannot describe a 10 m cell. At 30 m cells and larger it works, and Brazilian coffee blocks are normally coarser than that. Say so in the interface rather than implying precision the data does not hold.
Where the published evidence is strongest A 2024 drone study of frost‑damaged Brazilian coffee split its plots exactly this way: plants on slopes were the low‑risk group, plants in valley areas the high‑risk group, and plant age changed the damage within each. That is the same two variables this design uses, measured on the same crop in the same country.

5 · Sources

Studies and artifacts behind the numbers

Handbook Snyder & de Melo‑Abreu (2005), Frost Protection: fundamentals, practice and economics, FAO Environment and Natural Resources Series 10 · Chapter 5, forecasting Source of the two‑hours‑after‑sunset equation, the 0.65 °C worked‑example RMSE, and the note that growers historically subtracted 0.5 °C by hand for low‑lying ground.
Study Remotely Piloted Aircraft for Evaluating the Impact of Frost in Coffee Plants: Interactions between Plant Age and Topography, Remote Sensing 16(18), 2024 Brazilian coffee, measured. Splits risk by slope versus valley position and by plant age. The direct evidence for both the terrain offset and the age weighting.
Study Unmanned aerial vehicle to evaluate frost damage in coffee plants, Precision Agriculture 23, 2021 Method for scoring frost damage per plot from imagery. A route to ground truth that does not depend on the grower reporting.
Study Chung et al. (2006), Minimum temperature mapping over complex terrain by estimating cold air accumulation potential, Agricultural and Forest Meteorology 137, 15–24 The closest published analogue to Stage 3. Interpolates night minimum temperature using a terrain‑derived cold‑air‑accumulation term.
Study Lundquist et al. (2008), Automated algorithm for mapping regions of cold‑air pooling in complex terrain, JGR Atmospheres 113 A repeatable way to derive pooling zones from a DEM. Use this to define frost_offset_c rather than inventing a formula.
Study Rennó et al. (2008), HAND, a new terrain descriptor using SRTM‑DEM, Remote Sensing of Environment 112, 3469–3481 Defines height above the nearest drainage. Brazilian authors, Brazilian terrain, and the descriptor that best separates a valley‑bottom cell from a slope cell.
Data Copernicus DEM GLO‑30 30 m global elevation, free, covers Brazil. The proposed DEM source.
Data Open‑Meteo forecast API Already the platform’s provider. dew_point_2m, soil_temperature_0cm and soil_moisture_0_to_1cm are on the same endpoint already in use.
Data ECOSTRESS 70 m thermal from the space station, free, acquires at night over Brazil. Revisit is irregular, so it calibrates the offsets; it cannot warn.
Code The frost R package, buildFAO A working implementation of the FAO equation with fitted coefficients. Useful as a reference to check our own port against.
