Part 01

The Brazilian coffee sector

Brazil is the largest coffee producer in the world and has been for over a century. It grows both commercial species: Coffea arabica (arábica) on the highland plateaus of the south-east, and Coffea canephora, called conilon in Brazil and robusta elsewhere, in the warmer lowlands.

Read the numbers as orders of magnitude

The figures in this part come from general sector knowledge, not from a live data source. Before any of them goes into a customer deck, check them against CONAB (the government crop survey), the Ministry of Agriculture, and CECAFÉ (the exporters' council). Treat them as a shape, not as facts.

Scale

National output
Roughly 55–65 million bags a year, where a bag (saca) is 60 kg of green coffee.
Split by species
Roughly two thirds arábica, one third conilon. The conilon share has been rising.
Planted area
Roughly 2.2–2.3 million hectares in production.
Producing farms
Roughly 300,000. Most are under 20 ha. A small number of estates run 1,000–10,000 ha.

Where it grows

Production concentrates in a handful of states, and each one implies a different customer and a different agronomic problem.

The two structural forces to sell against

Biennial bearing. An arabica field alternates a heavy year and a light year. A heavy crop drains the plant's reserves, so the next year sets fewer nodes. Whole regions swing together, so national output swings too. Every yield conversation, every fertiliser plan and every baseline comparison in Brazil has to know which half of the cycle a block is in. This is not a detail. It is the organising fact of Brazilian coffee management.

Traceability to the plot. The European Union's deforestation regulation puts coffee in scope and requires operators to hold geolocation data for the plots of land where the coffee was produced, with polygons for larger plots, plus a due diligence statement. Brazil separately runs the Cadastro Ambiental Rural (CAR), a mandatory environmental registry of rural property boundaries. Both are polygon problems.

This is the wedge

AgriPulse already stores farms and blocks as real geometry, with per-block imagery and a full activity history. The compliance requirement gives a Brazilian exporter or cooperative a reason to draw every plot as a polygon in some system this year. Being that system is a cheaper first sale than "better agronomy", and it puts our blocks under their feet before we sell a single decision tree. Check the current application dates before quoting them; they have moved more than once.

Part 03

Coffee agronomy, in the terms the platform needs

The plant

Coffee is a perennial evergreen shrub, kept at 2–3.5 m and grown in rows. It starts producing in year 3 or 4, peaks around years 6 to 12, and is renewed by pruning rather than replanting. A block therefore has an age and a position in a pruning cycle, and both change what any measurement means. A first-year field after a stumping cut has almost no canopy and is perfectly healthy.

Cultivars

Cultivar choice drives disease resistance, ripening date, plant height and spacing. It is not cosmetic metadata. It is a targeting key.

Common cultivars and clone groups
SpeciesCultivar or groupNote
ArábicaCatuaí Vermelho and Amarelo (144, 62, 99)The workhorse. Short stature, suits dense and mechanised planting.
ArábicaMundo NovoTall, vigorous, high yielding, susceptible to rust.

Modelling problem: the mixed-clone block

A conilon block is deliberately planted with several clones that flower and ripen at different times. Our crop taxonomy assigns one crop path per block. A conilon field cannot honestly be coffee.conilon.vitoria, because it is six clones at once. Either the clone group becomes the variety (coffee.conilon.vitoria_group), or the block carries an attribute listing its clones. Decide this before the first conilon customer, because the choice reaches phenology, harvest planning and every decision tree.

Planting geometry

Spacing determines how much soil a satellite pixel sees, how much fertiliser a hectare needs, and whether a harvester can enter the row.

A known defect that will bite here

Plant density does not exist anywhere today, for any tenant. Migration 0061_drop_unused_block_crop_agronomy_columns removed plant_density_per_ha, row_spacing_m and plant_spacing_m from block_crops platform-wide, because nothing ever wrote them: every crop-assignment row in production was read first, and all five dropped columns held zero non-null values. The same migration released those codes from RESERVED_ATTRIBUTE_CODES, so they are now free to author in the crop-attribute system. Coffee makes that urgent, because per-plant rates are the normal way Brazilian recommendations are written: grams per plant, not kilograms per hectare. Density must be a required, validated crop attribute before the first Brazilian block is created.

Renewal pruning

Instead of replanting, growers cut the plant back and let it rebuild. This is a scheduled, capital-scale decision, and it produces a block that looks dead from orbit for a year.

  • Esqueletamento — lateral branches cut back to short stubs, main stem kept.
  • Decote — the top removed at a set height, often combined with skeletonisation.
  • Recepa — the whole plant cut near the ground. The most drastic renewal.
  • Safra zero — a deliberate no-crop year, usually the year after a heavy pruning.
Modelling problem: a healthy block with no canopy

A recently stumped block reads very low on every vegetation index for a full season. If the platform treats that as a health failure, we flood a Brazilian customer's Action Center with false criticals in their first month, which is the fastest way to lose the account. The pruning state has to suppress index-based health, not merely annotate it.

Harvest and post-harvest

Harvest quality is where Brazilian growers make or lose their margin, and it is decided by ripeness uniformity at the moment of picking.

  • Derriça — stripping all fruit off the branch at once, by hand or by machine. Because it takes everything, the block must be as uniformly ripe as possible before entry.
  • Colheita seletiva — picking only ripe cherries, over several passes. Used for specialty lots and on slopes.
  • Natural — whole cherry dried. The traditional Brazilian method.
  • Cereja descascado — pulped natural: skin removed, mucilage kept, then dried.
  • Despolpado and fermentado — fully washed or controlled fermentation, for specialty lots.
  • Secagem — dried on a patio (terreiro), on raised beds, or in a mechanical drier, down to about 11–12% moisture.

The commercial unit throughout is the saca of 60 kg of green coffee, and yields are quoted in sacas per hectare. Typical figures run about 25–45 sacas per hectare for well-managed rainfed arabica, higher under irrigation, and 40–80 for irrigated conilon.

Part 04

The coffee year, and why our calendar breaks

Brazil is in the southern hemisphere, so its season crosses the calendar-year boundary. Worse, an arabica crop takes about two years from bud initiation to harvest, so two crops sit on the same plant at all times. The standard Brazilian description, following Camargo and Camargo, uses six phenological phases across 24 months.

Arabica phenology in Brazil, with proposed stage codes
Months, approximatePhaseProposed stage_codeWhat management does
Sep–MarVegetative growth of buds and new nodesveg_growthNitrogen and potassium, weed control, leaf-miner watch
Apr–AugFloral bud induction and dormancybud_inductionHarvest happens here. Buds wait for a rain or irrigation trigger.
Sep–OctFlowering, triggered by rain after dry stressfloweringThe single most important date of the year
Oct–DecPinhead and fruit expansion (chumbinho)fruit_expansionWater is critical. Boron and calcium.
Jan–MarGrain filling (granação)grain_fillingPeak nutrient demand, peak rust pressure
Apr–JunRipening (maturação)ripeningRipeness monitoring, harvest sequencing
May–SepHarvest and post-harvest restharvest, post_harvest_restPicking, drying, renewal pruning, reserve-rebuilding fertiliser
Engineering gap: stages are anchored on growing degree days

Our phenology catalog stores stages as {stage, start_gdd, end_gdd} rows resolved through the crop taxonomy in backend/app/modules/farms/crop_thresholds.py. Coffee flowering is not a thermal-accumulation event. It is triggered by a rainfall or irrigation pulse of roughly 10–20 mm after a dry, cool induction period. A degree-day spine puts flowering on the wrong day, and everything anchored to it in a plan template is then wrong too.

Coffee needs an event-anchored stage: the flowering date is observed or detected, and the rest of the season is offset from it. Plan templates already support a milestone anchor alongside stage, which is the closest existing mechanism.

Engineering gap: several flowerings in one block

If the spring rains arrive in two or three separate pulses, the block flowers two or three times and carries several fruit cohorts at once. That is the classic Brazilian uneven-ripening problem, and it is why irrigated growers apply a deliberate controlled water stress and then one heavy irrigation, to force a single synchronised flowering. A model that assumes one flowering date per block per year cannot represent the problem the customer is paying to solve.

Part 05

Pests, diseases and climate risk

The five that matter

Main biotic problems and the conditions that drive them
ProblemOrganismFavoured byAction threshold in practice
Ferrugem, leaf rust Hemileia vastatrix Mean temperature around 21–25 °C, long leaf wetness, dense canopy, and above all a heavy fruit load, which weakens the plant A preventive programme from about December. Scouting incidence above roughly 5–10% of leaves triggers curative action.
Bicho-mineiro, leaf miner Leucoptera coffeella Hot, dry weather. Rain knocks larvae back, so it is the mirror image of rust. Roughly 20–30% of leaves mined
Broca-do-café, berry borer Hypothenemus hampei Fruit present at the right moisture. Carry-over in fruit left on the plant and ground after harvest. Roughly 3–5% of fruit bored
Cercosporiose, brown eye spot Cercospora coffeicola Nutritional deficiency, water stress, high light exposure, poor soil Treated as a nutrition signal as much as a disease
Nematoides Meloidogyne spp., Pratylenchus Infested soil and seedlings. Spreads outward from a focus. No spray fixes it. The response is resistant rootstock and containment.
Two good fits for what we already do

Nematodes show up as patches. They spread as expanding foci, not uniformly. That is precisely the shape our per-cell grid anomaly detection finds: a persistent low-index cluster inside an otherwise normal block. A Brazilian agronomist will recognise that picture immediately.

Rust and leaf miner move in opposite directions on the same weather. A wet fortnight raises rust and suppresses leaf miner. A dry hot fortnight does the reverse. Running both risk models side by side gives a two-sided reading from one data source.

Climate risk

  • Geada, frost. The defining catastrophic risk of Sul de Minas, Mogiana and Paraná, from June to August. Radiative frost forms on clear, calm nights, and cold air drains downhill, so the bottom of a valley freezes while the ridge above it does not. Damage runs from leaf scorch to losing the plant to the ground.
  • Veranico, a dry spell. A rain-free hot period inside the wet season, most damaging during fruit expansion.
  • Heat at flowering. Temperatures above roughly 34 °C during flowering abort flowers and produce hollow beans (grãos-chochos).
  • Granizo, hail. Local and abrupt. A satellite pass after the event is the cheapest damage assessment available.
  • Water allocation. In Espírito Santo and parts of Bahia, irrigation abstraction is regulated and has been restricted in drought years, which turns water efficiency from an economy into a compliance question.
Engineering gap: frost is a terrain problem

A block-mean minimum temperature from a weather grid cannot represent cold-air drainage. Frost risk needs elevation, and ideally terrain position within the block. Both farms and blocks carry an elevation_m column, but that is one number per polygon, not a terrain surface, and neither is exposed to a decision tree: BLOCK_FIELDS holds only growth_stage, soil_texture and salinity_class. So a tree cannot read elevation at all today, and nothing we store describes cold-air drainage inside a block. A first version can be honest about that: use the forecast minimum temperature at the block, band it, and say plainly that low ground inside the block will be colder than the figure shown. Do not present one number as if it applied uniformly.

Part 07

Imagery and indices over coffee

Coffee is harder to read from orbit than a broadacre crop, for three reasons that are all geometric.

  1. Rows of bushes with bare soil between them. A Sentinel-2 pixel is 10 m; coffee rows sit 3.5–4 m apart. Every pixel mixes canopy and inter-row soil, so a healthy mechanised block reads much lower than a healthy closed-canopy field, and a dense planting reads much higher than a wide one. Absolute index thresholds are meaningless across blocks of different spacing.
  2. An evergreen canopy. There is no bare-soil-to-full-canopy curve to anchor a season against. The signal is a slow drift with a seasonal wobble.
  3. Fruit is invisible. Yield sits under the canopy. No optical index sees cherries. Anything we say about yield is inferred from canopy vigour, weather and the biennial cycle, and we have to say so.
Index behaviour on coffee

savi, msaviRed, NIRSoil-adjusted, which is exactly the row-crop mixing problem. Preferable to NDVI on wide-spaced blocks.

Engineering gap: health is off NDVI already, but behind a flag that is off

Health no longer has to come from an index. backend/app/shared/health_definition.py resolves a block's class from decision-tree alert output through five bounded values: severity_map, snoozed_as, cell_critical_share, recommendation_floor and stale_after_hours. No index appears in it. The chain is the platform default, then a per-crop row in public.crop_health_definitions targeted by crop path, then a per-farm override in farms.health_definition. The frontend copy of the old rule is deleted; frontend/src/modules/labs/map/health.ts now carries the palette only.

The setting health_definition_enabled defaults to false. While it is off, the old rule in backend/app/shared/health.py is what runs: below 0.40 critical, below 0.55 watch, for every crop. A wide-spaced mechanised coffee block sits under 0.55 while perfectly healthy, and a recently stumped block sits under 0.40. So the coffee work here is to turn the flag on and add a coffee.yaml seed to backend/app/modules/health/seeds/, not to build a per-crop threshold system.

One route from an index to health survives the switch. An index-driven tree still opens an alert, and the alert's severity still sets the class through severity_map. A stumped coffee block therefore still reads critical if an NDVI or NDRE tree fires on it. That is tree authoring, not a platform change.

Engineering gap: baselines and biennial bearing

The index baseline needs at least three samples within seven days either side of a day of year before it produces a deviation score for that day, so a new farm carries null z-scores until the history builds. With coffee that is worse than slow. The alternating heavy and light years mean a two-year sample compares a heavy year against a light year, and the deviation is real but meaningless. A coffee baseline should compare like with like, heavy year against heavy years, which needs either four years of history or a baseline that keys on the bearing_year attribute.

Adding a rust risk model

The weather risk registry in backend/app/modules/weather/risk/registry.py is deliberately append-only. It holds three mango models today. A new pathogen is one RiskModelSpec entry plus one pure function: no schema change, no task change, no endpoint change. Crop applicability is a taxonomy path prefix, so one entry covers every arabica cultivar.

# backend/app/modules/weather/risk/registry.py
REGISTRY: tuple[RiskModelSpec, ...] = (
    RiskModelSpec("powdery_mildew", "mango", "disease", models.powdery_mildew),
    RiskModelSpec("anthracnose",    "mango", "disease", models.anthracnose),
    RiskModelSpec("fruit_fly",      "mango", "pest",    models.fruit_fly),
    # --- proposed, Brazil coffee ---
    RiskModelSpec("coffee_rust", "coffee.arabica", "disease",
                  models.coffee_rust, window_days=30),
    RiskModelSpec("leaf_miner",  "coffee",         "pest",
                  models.leaf_miner),
)

The model function integrates favourable conditions over a trailing window into a score from 0 to 100, banded low, moderate and high at cutoffs of 40 and 70. For rust, the favourable-condition count is warm and wet days, and the fruit load pushes the score upward. That last term is what makes it a coffee model rather than a generic wet-weather model.

The score is 0 to 100, not 0 to 1

Every threshold written into a coffee tree's parameters must be on the 0 to 100 scale. A tree that compares a risk score against 0.7 will never fire, and will never report an error.

A worked tree, in the real seed format

code: coffee_rust_risk_v1
name_en: Coffee — leaf rust risk
crop_path: coffee.arabica

evidence:
  confidence: medium
  notes: >-
    Hemileia vastatrix needs warm temperatures with prolonged leaf
    wetness, and pressure rises sharply on plants carrying a heavy
    fruit load. The score accumulates favourable days; it is not a
    validated infection model, so the tree recommends scouting and a
    protective decision rather than a blind application.

parameters:
  warn_score: { type: number, default: 40, min: 0, max: 100 }
  high_score: { type: number, default: 70, min: 0, max: 100 }

root: root
nodes:
  root:
    label_en: Is rust pressure elevated?
    condition:
      tree:
        op: ge
        left:  { source: weather_risk, risk_code: coffee_rust, field: score }
        right: { source: params, name: warn_score }
    on_match: bearing_check
    on_miss:  leaf_no_action

  bearing_check:
    label_en: Is this a high-bearing year?
    condition:
      tree:
        op: eq
        left:  { source: crop_attribute, code: bearing_year, key: value }
        right: "high"
    on_match: leaf_critical
    on_miss:  leaf_warning

The comparison operators the evaluator accepts are lt, le, gt, ge, eq, ne, plus in and between. The right-hand side is either a plain literal, as above, or a params reference. Note that between does not take right: it takes low and high.

Two traps this codebase has already fallen into

An unknown operator raises, but an unresolved reference does not. Writing gte where the evaluator expects ge raises ConditionParseError at backend/app/shared/conditions/evaluator.py:140, so that mistake is caught. The quiet failure is the other one: when a reference resolves to nothing, the comparison returns "did not match" with no error at all. A misspelled crop_attribute code, a risk row the model never wrote, and a missing parameter all take on_miss for ever. Check every attribute code and risk code in a coffee seed against the data, not only the operators against the evaluator's list.

A tree that is never selected reports success. The mango index-guide sweep evaluated no tree at all in production, because the package selection named the wrong thing, and nothing reported a failure. After seeding the coffee trees, confirm that evaluation traces exist for real blocks. A run that reports "0 opened" is not by itself evidence that the trees ran.

Adding a third *_pt column set would be the wrong answer twice over, because a fourth language would then need a third migration. Large. A data-model change, not a translation job.
B2 Turn on the health definition and seed coffee While health_definition_enabled is off, the fixed NDVI cutoffs mislabel wide-spaced and recently pruned coffee. The replacement is already merged, so this is enabling a setting and adding a coffee row to the crop health catalog, not building a threshold system. Warn first: a tenant whose sweep does not reach every block will see Unknown where it saw Healthy. Small. A setting and one seed file.
B3 Southern-hemisphere season handling A season that crosses the calendar year must not be split at 31 December in plans, reports or baselines. The platform has no formal season entity today, and four parts of the system pick a season start independently. Medium. Needs the season question settled first.
B4 Brazilian units and formats Saca of 60 kg, sacas per hectare, comma decimal separator, BRL, CPF and CNPJ identifiers, and CAR property codes. Small to medium
B5 Pruning state gates the index trees A stumped block reads low on every index for a season. Health itself stops reading NDVI once B2 lands, but an index tree that fires still opens an alert, and the alert still sets the class. The index trees must gate on last_pruning_date. Small. Tree authoring, not platform work.

High value, not blocking

  • Rust and leaf-miner risk models in the weather risk registry. Two registry entries and two pure functions. The best return on effort in the whole list.
  • Event-anchored phenology, so the flowering date can be observed or detected and the rest of the season offset from it.
  • Bearing-year-aware baselines, comparing a heavy year against heavy years.
  • A coffee cultivar catalogue, seeded with the common Catuaí, Mundo Novo, Catucaí, Arara and IPR lines and the main conilon clone groups, each carrying rust resistance and ripening class.

Commercially strategic

  • Plot polygon export for compliance. Geometry per plot, area, farm identifier and a stable plot code, in the shape the customer's due-diligence filing needs. We hold the data already. This is a report, not a platform change.
  • Attachment upload. Photographs of rust lesions, borer damage and frost injury are how a Brazilian field agent communicates. Browser uploads to our object storage have never worked in production, for want of a CORS policy, and there are zero attachment rows in any production tenant. On a photo-driven crop that stops being a latent bug and becomes a blocker.
  • LGPD review. Brazil's data protection law covers the personal data of members and workers, which a cooperative deployment holds a great deal of.

Open questions

  1. Mixed-clone conilon blocks. Does the clone group become the variety, or does the block carry a list attribute? This decision sits upstream of phenology and harvest planning.
  2. How is Portuguese added? A third column set is quick and wrong. A locale table is right and large. Which one, and does the same decision unblock Spanish for the rest of Latin America?
  3. Do we take on frost properly? Doing it well needs terrain data we do not have. Doing it badly, on a crop where frost is the catastrophic risk, damages trust.
  4. What does the platform promise about yield? Brazilian growers expect a forecast in sacas per hectare, and no optical index sees fruit. Either we build a forecast that combines vigour, weather and bearing year and state its uncertainty, or we say clearly that we do not forecast yield.
  5. Is the season entity now unavoidable? A crop whose cycle crosses the calendar year, with a two-year overlap between successive crops, is a harder case than anything the platform carries today.
  6. Who is the agronomic authority? Every threshold in this document is a rule of thumb from extension literature. Brazil has a strong public research base: Embrapa, IAC, INCAPER, IAPAR, Fundação Procafé and the Cerrado cooperatives. One named Brazilian agronomist reviewing the seeds is worth more than any amount of tuning.
